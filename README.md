# U.S.–Mexico Tariff Trade Analysis Project

## Overview

This project analyzes tariff and trade relationships between the United States and Mexico using real 2022 trade data from the World Integrated Trade Solution (WITS) database. The objective of the project was to evaluate whether U.S. exports are unfairly taxed abroad by comparing tariff revenue, import/export volume, and product-level trade patterns between the two countries.

Using R programming and data visualization techniques, the analysis explores how tariff revenue differs across product categories and whether trade imbalances are caused primarily by tariff policy or by differences in trade volume. Multiple visualizations were created to compare U.S. imports from Mexico, U.S. exports to Mexico, and tariff revenue collected by both nations.

The findings suggest that while tariff imbalances do exist in certain product groups, the United States generally collects more tariff revenue from Mexican imports due to larger import quantities rather than significantly higher tariff rates. Product categories such as consumer goods and capital goods contributed the most to trade volume and tariff revenue. The analysis ultimately concludes that trade volume differences, rather than unfair tariff policies, are the primary driver of the imbalance observed in U.S.–Mexico trade relations.

This project demonstrates the use of economic data analysis, quantitative reasoning, and data visualization techniques to evaluate international trade relationships and interpret policy-related economic trends.

## Research Question

Are U.S. exports being unfairly taxed compared to imports entering the United States from Mexico?

This project investigates whether tariff imbalances are primarily caused by:

* Unfair tariff policies
* Differences in import/export volume
* Product-specific trade relationships
* Trade concentration among certain industries

## Dataset Information

The dataset contains 2022 U.S.–Mexico trade and tariff data sourced from the World Integrated Trade Solution (WITS) database, including:

* Product groups
* Tariff revenue
* Import values
* Export values
* Country-level trade comparisons

The dataset was analyzed in R through summary tables and multiple visualizations to better understand trade imbalances and tariff patterns.

## Key Analyses Performed

### Tariff Revenue Analysis

The project compares tariff revenue collected by the United States and Mexico across various product groups to determine whether one country benefits more heavily from tariff collection.

### Import and Export Analysis

Trade volume between the two countries was analyzed to identify which product categories contributed most heavily to imports and exports.

### Product Group Comparisons

The analysis examined whether certain industries demonstrated stronger trade imbalances or unusually high tariff revenue.
---

## Key Visualizations

### Tariff Revenue by Country and Product Group

A line graph was created to compare tariff revenue collected by the United States and Mexico across multiple product groups.

The analysis showed that the United States generally collected more tariff revenue due to larger import volumes, although Mexico exceeded the United States in a few product-specific categories.

### U.S. Imports from Mexico by Product Group

A horizontal bar chart analyzed which product categories contributed the most to U.S. imports from Mexico.

The visualization demonstrated that consumer products and capital goods represented the largest share of imports and generated the highest tariff revenue.

### U.S.–Mexico Trade by Product Group

A grouped bar chart compared U.S. imports from Mexico against U.S. exports to Mexico across product categories.

The visualization revealed a significant trade imbalance favoring Mexican exports in several major product groups.

---

## Key Findings

* The United States generally collects more tariff revenue from Mexican imports than Mexico collects from U.S. exports.
* Trade imbalances are primarily driven by higher import volumes rather than significantly different tariff rates.
* Consumer goods and capital goods account for the largest share of trade volume and tariff revenue.
* Certain product groups show stronger imbalances than others, indicating industry-specific trade concentration.
* The data does not strongly support the claim that the United States is systematically “tariffed more” than Mexico in bilateral trade.

## Conclusion

This project demonstrates how trade and tariff data can be analyzed to evaluate economic relationships between countries. The findings suggest that tariff imbalances between the United States and Mexico are primarily influenced by differences in trade volume rather than unfair tariff policies. Through product-level analysis and data visualization, the project highlights the importance of interpreting international trade data within broader economic contexts rather than relying solely on political assumptions regarding tariffs and trade fairness.
